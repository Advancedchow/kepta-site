Half a Jar of Spoiled Sauce: What to Do With the Food and the Jar

You open the fridge, find half a jar of pasta sauce that is past saving, and stand there holding it. Two different things need a home: the sauce and the jar. They go to different places, and the sauce always comes out first.

Why the food comes out first

Food left inside a container is one of the main ways recycling goes wrong. The Recycling Partnership estimated that about 17 percent of what arrives at sorting plants is contamination, a 2020 figure (Waste Today). Cities warn against "wishcycling," tossing something in and hoping, because one bad item can send a whole load to the landfill (UT Austin, UW).

The bar is lower than people think. The standard is "spatula clean": scrape out what you can, and a quick rinse is good (EPA, UT Austin). You don't need to scrub the jar until it squeaks.

Where the sauce goes

All food is biodegradable, but where it ends up decides whether that helps. In a compost pile it becomes soil. In a landfill it rots without air and makes methane, and the EPA estimates food waste is about 58 percent of the methane that escapes from municipal landfills (EPA).

  • A sauce that is mostly tomatoes and vegetables is closest to vegetable scraps, which can go in a home compost pile or your town's food scrap bin. Otherwise, the trash (EPA, NYC Sanitation).
  • A meat sauce, a cream sauce, or one heavy with cheese or oil belongs with meat, dairy and fats, which the EPA keeps out of a backyard pile. Many city food scrap programs do take them; New York City accepts meat, bones and dairy. Otherwise, the trash (EPA, NYC Sanitation).
  • Not down the sink. The EPA's Wasted Food Scale puts sending food down the drain near the bottom, next to the landfill, because it "decays in sewers and produces methane" (EPA). If there's a layer of oil on top, the rule is firmer still: grease never goes down the drain (City of Sacramento).

Where the jar goes

Here the honest answer is to check your town. Glass is one of the clearest examples of how local recycling is. Rumpke, which serves Cincinnati and the Columbus area, takes glass bottles and jars of all colors (Rumpke). Several other cities, Fargo-Moorhead among them in 2023, moved glass out of curbside bins to drop-off sites only, citing cost and contamination, while Pittsburgh still takes it at the curb (MPR News, RecycleNation).

  • Scrape and rinse the jar.
  • Take the metal lid off. The EPA says to recycle metal caps separately, with metal (EPA).
  • The label can usually stay on (EPA, Washington County, Oregon).
  • If your town takes glass at the curb, the jar goes into the bin loose, not in a bag (UT Austin, Rumpke).
  • If it doesn't, look for a glass drop-off site on your city or county website.

A broken jar is a different item. Broken glass, drinking glasses, Pyrex and ceramics don't go in with jars and bottles (EPA, Washington County, Oregon), and in most places they go in the trash.

The short version

Scrape the sauce into compost, a food scrap bin, or the trash. Rinse the jar. The lid goes with metal, and the jar goes with glass if your town takes it. When you're not sure, search your city or county website for "recycling," or try Earth911's search in the US. If it's still unclear, the advice cities give is "when in doubt, throw it out" (UT Austin).
